\chapter{Needs across life stages}

\section{Learning objectives}
Relate nutrient requirements to growth, pregnancy, lactation, aging, and training; identify groups at risk of deficiency; and select food-first and supplement strategies appropriate to the life stage.

\section{Clinical principles}
Life-stage recommendations are only useful when affordable, acceptable, and accessible. Assessment should include customary foods, cooking facilities, religious restrictions, disability, food security, and medicines. Growth, pregnancy, lactation, aging, and training change physiology, but they do not justify indiscriminate megadosing.

\section{Pregnancy and breastfeeding}
Pregnancy increases demands for folate, iron, iodine, choline, and several other nutrients. A prenatal supplement can be useful, but formulations vary and should be reviewed for vitamin A form and dose, iron tolerance, iodine, and folic acid. Avoid herbal megablends and retinol-heavy products unless prescribed. Breastfeeding increases some needs and makes maternal diet important, but breast milk nutrient content is not a reason to take every supplement.

\section{Infants and children}
Infants have distinct needs and should not receive adult supplements. Breastfed infants commonly need vitamin D supplementation according to local pediatric guidance; iron timing depends on gestation, feeding pattern, and clinical advice. Keep iron, vitamin gummies, and supplements locked away: accidental ingestion can be life-threatening. Growth, appetite, and developmental concerns deserve pediatric assessment rather than empiric megadoses.

\section{Older adults}
Absorption, appetite, sunlight exposure, thirst, and medication use can change with age. Attention to protein, B12, vitamin D, calcium, and hydration is often useful, but routine high-dose supplementation is not automatically beneficial. Fortified foods and appropriately chosen low-dose supplements may be easier to tolerate than large tablets.

\section{Vegetarian and vegan patterns}
Well-planned plant-based diets can supply protein, fiber, folate, vitamin C, magnesium, potassium, and many other nutrients. Reliable B12 is essential for people avoiding animal foods. Iron, zinc, iodine, calcium, vitamin D, and omega-3 fats may need deliberate planning; fortified foods and targeted supplements can help.

\section{Athletes and physically active people}
Training increases energy and fluid needs, but not necessarily every micronutrient requirement. Sweat sodium losses vary with climate, acclimatization, body size, pace, and individual sweat composition. For prolonged endurance activity, an individualized hydration plan is safer than drinking unlimited water or taking concentrated electrolyte products.
Sweat losses, energy expenditure, and restrictive eating can raise risk of inadequate intake. A balanced diet and adequate fluids usually matter more than a large micronutrient stack. Recurrent fatigue, injuries, menstrual disruption, or reduced performance should prompt assessment for inadequate energy, iron deficiency, illness, and training load.
