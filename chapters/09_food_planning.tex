\chapter{Building a nutrient-dense eating pattern}
\section{Learning objectives}
Translate nutrient science into culturally appropriate meals; identify barriers to adequacy; plan food substitutions; and avoid reducing health to a single nutrient or ``superfood.''

\section{A flexible plate}
At most meals, combine vegetables or fruit, a protein-rich food, a whole-grain or starchy food, and a source of unsaturated fat. Rotate foods rather than searching for one perfect ingredient. Beans, lentils, tofu, eggs, fish, dairy or fortified alternatives, nuts, seeds, vegetables, fruit, and whole grains together cover a broad nutrient base.

\section{Practical upgrades}
Diet quality is a pattern rather than a checklist. Adequacy depends on total energy, protein, fiber, essential fatty acids, and micronutrients, while health outcomes are also influenced by sodium, added sugars, alcohol, food processing, and physical activity.
\begin{itemize}
\item Add beans or lentils to soups, salads, and grain dishes for folate, iron, magnesium, potassium, and protein.
\item Use iodized salt in modest amounts if appropriate for your household; do not assume specialty salts contain iodine.
\item Pair plant iron sources with peppers, citrus, tomatoes, or other vitamin-C foods.
\item Choose fortified foods deliberately, especially for B12, vitamin D, calcium, and iodine when your diet excludes common sources.
\item Preserve variety across the week: dark greens, orange produce, legumes, whole grains, nuts and seeds, and protein foods.
\end{itemize}

\section{Food safety and supplements}
Cooking and storage can change vitamin content, but raw is not always better. Wash produce, avoid unsafe raw animal foods, and follow local advice for fish choices in pregnancy. Supplements cannot compensate for inadequate calories, protein, sleep, or medical care.

\section{A one-day template}
Breakfast might include fortified oatmeal, fruit, and nuts; lunch could be a lentil and vegetable bowl with yogurt or a fortified alternative; dinner could combine fish, tofu, or beans with vegetables and a whole grain. Snacks can include fruit, roasted chickpeas, or seeds. This is a template, not a prescription: adapt it to culture, budget, allergies, ethics, and medical needs.
