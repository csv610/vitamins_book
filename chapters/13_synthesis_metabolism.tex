\chapter{How vitamins and minerals are synthesized}

\section{Learning objectives}
Distinguish endogenous synthesis from activation and recycling; identify the limited vitamin pathways humans possess; explain the role of plants and microorganisms; and understand why minerals are obtained from the environment rather than synthesized by the body.

\section{What ``synthesis'' means in nutrition}
In nutrition, synthesis may refer to several different processes. A cell may build a vitamin molecule from a precursor, convert an ingested precursor into an active form, recycle a cofactor after it has performed its function, or obtain a compound made by microorganisms. These processes are not interchangeable. A precursor pathway may be insufficient under ordinary conditions, and an activated metabolite may have a different regulatory role from the dietary form.

\section{Human synthesis and activation}
Humans cannot synthesize most vitamins in sufficient amounts and therefore require dietary sources. Important exceptions and partial exceptions include vitamin D synthesis in skin, endogenous conversion of tryptophan to niacin, and limited contributions from intestinal microorganisms. Vitamin A can be formed from provitamin A carotenoids, but conversion varies and does not make carotenoid intake equivalent to preformed retinol. Vitamin K forms may be produced by gut bacteria, although the amount absorbed and its contribution to total requirement are uncertain. \cite{vitaminDMetabolism,niacinDRI,vitaminKMetabolism}

Vitamin D provides the clearest example of endogenous synthesis. Ultraviolet B radiation converts 7-dehydrocholesterol in the epidermis to previtamin D3, which rearranges to cholecalciferol (vitamin D3). The liver hydroxylates it to 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the principal circulating status metabolite. The kidney and some other tissues can hydroxylate this to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), a hormone-like regulator of calcium and phosphate physiology. Skin pigmentation, age, latitude, season, clothing, sunscreen, and indoor living alter production; sun exposure also carries skin-cancer risk, so it should not be prescribed casually as a vitamin dose. \cite{vitaminDMetabolism}

\section{Plant and microbial biosynthesis}
Plants, algae, fungi, and bacteria synthesize many vitamins that enter human food chains. Plants make carotenoids and folates; yeasts and fungi can make or accumulate B vitamins; bacteria synthesize some cobalamin-related compounds and menaquinones. Food processing, fermentation, storage, soil, light, and cooking influence the final amount. Fermentation may improve digestibility or create a bioavailable form, but it is not a guarantee that a food supplies a complete requirement.

\section{Vitamin activation and coenzyme formation}
After absorption, vitamins often undergo phosphorylation, methylation, hydroxylation, or conjugation. Thiamin becomes thiamin diphosphate, riboflavin becomes FMN and FAD, niacin becomes NAD and NADP, and B6 becomes pyridoxal phosphate. Folate is converted into tetrahydrofolate derivatives that carry one-carbon units. B12 cofactors support methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. These reactions explain why liver, kidney, intestine, and mitochondria are clinically relevant when organ disease alters nutrient metabolism.

\section{Minerals are not synthesized}
Minerals are chemical elements, not organic molecules. The human body cannot manufacture iron, calcium, iodine, magnesium, zinc, selenium, or potassium from other substances. It can absorb, transport, store, exchange, bind, and excrete them, and it can change their chemical state or incorporation into proteins. Plants acquire minerals from soil and water; animals obtain them through food. Geology, agriculture, fortification, water treatment, and food processing therefore influence mineral exposure.

\section{Recycling and conservation}
The body conserves some nutrients efficiently. Iron is recovered from senescent red cells and exported or stored through transferrin and ferritin. Vitamin K is repeatedly used in the clotting cycle and regenerated by vitamin K epoxide reductase, the pathway inhibited by warfarin. Folate is retained and transported through cellular folate pools. These conservation systems reduce daily requirements but also mean that disease, blood loss, inflammation, or medication can disrupt apparent status.

\section{Industrial production}
Commercial vitamins may be made by chemical synthesis, microbial fermentation, extraction from natural materials, or a combination of these methods. Manufacturing determines stereochemistry, purity, stability, excipients, and bioavailability. Vitamin D2 is commonly produced through irradiation of ergosterol, while vitamin D3 can be produced from cholesterol-related precursors; both enter supplements and fortified foods. A label's source does not by itself establish superior clinical efficacy. Quality control, dose accuracy, contaminants, and regulatory oversight matter more than the word ``natural.''

\section{Health effects of impaired synthesis}
Reduced skin production of vitamin D, impaired hepatic hydroxylation, renal failure, malabsorption, inflammation, genetic enzyme defects, and drug inhibition can all produce functional deficiency despite reasonable intake. Clinical management should identify the blocked step and choose a form that can be absorbed and activated safely. Giving more precursor may not correct a downstream enzyme defect and can increase toxicity.

\section{Clinical case}
A housebound older adult has low serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D and chronic kidney disease. The relevant question is not simply whether to take more dietary vitamin D. Assessment should include calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone, kidney function, medications, fracture risk, and the clinician's chosen replacement form. This illustrates the difference between nutritional intake, endogenous synthesis, activation, and treatment of a metabolic disorder.

\section{Summary}
Most vitamins must be obtained from food because human synthesis is absent or inadequate. The important exceptions are limited and regulated by physiology. Minerals cannot be synthesized at all. Health depends on the entire chain from environmental production and food preparation to intestinal absorption, cellular activation, recycling, and excretion.
